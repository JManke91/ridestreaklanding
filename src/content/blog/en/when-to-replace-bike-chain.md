The short answer: **a bike chain lasts 3,000 to 5,000 km, depending on conditions.** Ride in the wet, through winter, or push hard on small sprockets and you are at the bottom of that range. Ride dry and clean and lube regularly and you reach the top.

The odometer is the prompt to measure, not the proof. But it is the prompt most people miss — and that is where the bill comes from.

## Why the chain is the most expensive thing to put off

A chain costs €25 to €60. A cassette €40 to €250. Chainrings more.

A chain does not really "stretch" — the pins and bushings wear, and the spacing between links grows. Once that spacing no longer matches the teeth, the chain cuts its own shape into your cassette and chainrings. Past that point a new chain is not enough: it skips on the worn cassette, and you replace both.

Replaced in time, a cassette survives three or four chains. Replaced too late, it survives one. That is the whole difference, and it is why counting is worth it.

## Guide figures per component

These are the ranges Ride Streak uses as defaults in the Garage. They are starting points, not laws — after two or three replacements you will know your own numbers better.

| Component | Guide figure | What pulls it down |
|---|---|---|
| Chain | 3,000–5,000 km | Wet, salt, dust, infrequent lubing |
| Cassette | 10,000–15,000 km | Chains replaced too late |
| Brake pads | 5,000–8,000 km | Wet, long descents, high system weight |
| Tires | 5,000–8,000 km | Rear wears out far sooner than front |
| Shift and brake cables | 5,000–10,000 km | Internal routing, winter, road salt |
| Bar tape | 5,000–10,000 km | Purely comfort and looks |
| Brake rotors | 15,000–30,000 km | Watch the minimum thickness, usually 1.5 mm |

For tires and brake pads: count them separately. The rear tire carries around 60% of the weight and is often through twice as fast as the front. Track both as one component and you always replace one too early or one too late.

## The check: a chain gauge

Kilometres tell you *when to look*. The gauge tells you *whether it is time*. It costs under €15 and measures elongation as a percentage:

- **under 0.5%** — fine
- **0.5%** — replacement point for 11-, 12- and 13-speed drivetrains
- **0.75%** — replacement point for 8- to 10-speed
- **over 1%** — the cassette is very likely due as well

The narrower the chain, the less margin. Modern 12-speed drivetrains are far less forgiving than an old 9-speed setup.

Without a gauge there is a rough check: put the chain on the big ring and pull it away from the ring at the front. If you can lift it far enough to expose a whole tooth, it is long overdue.

## Why the kilometres are hard to keep track of

The guide figures are the easy part. The hard part is knowing **how many kilometres this particular part on this particular bike has seen since it was installed.**

Total odometer does not help: you ride several bikes, the chain was swapped mid-season, the tires a month later, and the rear pads are still the originals. It ends up on a note in the toolbox or nowhere at all — and things get replaced when they squeal or skip. Which is too late.
